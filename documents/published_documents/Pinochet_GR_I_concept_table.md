| No. | Concept | Category | Definition |
|-----|---------|----------|------------|
| 1 | General Relativity (GR) | Foundations & Historical Context | "The best available theory of gravity." A geometric theory of gravity that "describes the interaction between massive bodies as an effect of the curvature of spacetime." GR is built on Riemannian geometry and generalizes special relativity to curved spacetime. |
| 2 | First Golden Age of GR | Foundations & Historical Context | The period beginning in the 1960s when GR "underwent a renaissance." Kip Thorne's term for an era when "new mathematical techniques were developed that made calculations easier, technology had advanced enough to allow accurate tests of GR to be carried out, and astronomical observations were beginning to reveal extreme phenomena that could only be adequately explained in the framework of GR (such as pulsars and quasars)." |
| 3 | Second Golden Age of GR | Foundations & Historical Context | The recent period marked by three milestones: (1) first detection of gravitational waves by LIGO (2015), (2) first image of a black hole by EHT (2019), (3) image of the black hole at the centre of our galaxy by EHT (2022). These "spectacular proofs of Einstein's theory have aroused interest that goes beyond the narrow circle of specialists." |
| 4 | Euclidean Geometry | Riemann Geometry & Metric | "The branch of mathematics that studies the properties of flat spaces." Characterized by satisfying the parallel postulate. Riemannian geometry includes Euclidean geometry "as a particular case of zero curvature." |
| 5 | Non-Euclidean Geometry | Riemann Geometry & Metric | The branch of mathematics that describes "curved spaces." Does not satisfy the parallel postulate. The study of non-Euclidean geometries on curved surfaces "began in the 19th century with the work of Lobachevski, Bolyai, and Gauss" and was "generalised by Riemann for the study of curved spaces in any dimension." |
| 6 | Parallel Postulate | Riemann Geometry & Metric | "Two initially parallel lines that are prolonged indefinitely maintain their mutual distance constant." The essential difference between Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometry: "only Euclidean geometry satisfies the parallel postulate." |
| 7 | Manifold | Riemann Geometry & Metric | "The fundamental research object of Riemann's geometry." It "generalizes the notions of curve (one-dimensional manifold) and surfaces (two-dimensional manifold) to spaces having more than two dimensions (n-dimensional manifolds)." |
| 8 | Local Flatness Principle | Riemann Geometry & Metric | "In a small neighbourhood of a point, non-Euclidean manifolds agree with Euclidean geometry." Equivalently: "a sufficiently small region of curved space can be considered flat to a first approximation." An important corollary: "the Pythagorean theorem of Euclidean geometry holds locally on a non-Euclidean manifold." |
| 9 | Metric (Line Element dl) | Riemann Geometry & Metric | "The fundamental mathematical object of Riemannian geometry" — "a powerful generalisation of the Pythagorean theorem." The metric "allows the calculation of the infinitesimal distance or line element dl between two points on any n-dimensional manifold": (dl)² = Σ_i Σ_j g_ij dx_i dx_j. "All the properties of a manifold can be calculated from the metric or the metric tensor." |
| 10 | Metric Tensor g_ij | Riemann Geometry & Metric | "A set of quantities known as the metric tensor, which are functions of the coordinates and contain all the information about the manifold." Represented by an n×n square matrix with n² components. The metric tensor is "used to perform coordinate transformations." |
| 11 | Invariance of dl | Riemann Geometry & Metric | "dl is an invariant, that is, the infinitesimal distance between two points cannot depend on the chosen coordinate system." A line element dl written in one coordinate system can be transformed to dl′ in another under the condition dl = dl′. |
| 12 | Symmetry of Metric Tensor | Riemann Geometry & Metric | "The components under the main diagonal are equal to the components on said diagonal (g_ij = g_ji)." This symmetry reduces the number of independent components below n². |
| 13 | Independent Components N | Riemann Geometry & Metric | Due to symmetry (g_ij = g_ji), the number N of independent components is: N = 1+2+...+n = (1/2)n(n+1). For n=2: N=3; for n=3: N=6; for n=4: N=10. |
| 14 | Flat 2D Cartesian Metric | Riemann Geometry & Metric | For a two-dimensional Euclidean manifold (plane) in Cartesian coordinates x,y: g_11=g_22=1, g_12=g_21=0, yielding dl² = dx² + dy² — the Pythagorean theorem in two dimensions. |
| 15 | Flat 2D Polar Metric | Riemann Geometry & Metric | For a two-dimensional Euclidean manifold (plane) in polar coordinates r,φ: g_11=1, g_22=r², g_12=g_21=0, yielding dl² = dr² + r²dφ². Describes the same manifold as the Cartesian metric and can be transformed to/from it. |
| 16 | Curved 2D Spherical Surface Metric | Riemann Geometry & Metric | For the two-dimensional surface of a sphere (a curved manifold) with constant radius r in spherical coordinates θ,φ: g_11=r², g_22=r²sin²θ, g_12=g_21=0, yielding dl² = r²dθ² + r²sin²θ dφ². Although the surface is curved, "this metric was obtained using the Pythagorean theorem of plane geometry," applying the local flatness principle. |
| 17 | Flat 3D Cartesian Metric | Riemann Geometry & Metric | For a three-dimensional Euclidean manifold in Cartesian coordinates x,y,z: g_11=g_22=g_33=1, g_ij=0 if i≠j, yielding dl² = dx² + dy² + dz² — the Pythagorean theorem in three dimensions. |
| 18 | Flat 3D Spherical Metric | Riemann Geometry & Metric | For a three-dimensional Euclidean manifold in spherical coordinates r,θ,φ (r variable): g_11=1, g_22=r², g_33=r²sin²θ, g_ij=0 if i≠j, yielding dl² = dr² + r²dθ² + r²sin²θ dφ². If r=constant (dr=0), the spherical surface metric is recovered. |
| 19 | Zero-Curvature Diagonal Form | Riemann Geometry & Metric | "When the curvature is zero at every point of the manifold, it is always possible to find a coordinate system where g_ij = 1 if i=j, and g_ij = 0 if i≠j," so that the metric tensor takes the simple diagonal form: the n×n identity matrix. |
| 20 | Diagonal vs Non-Diagonal Metric Tensor | Riemann Geometry & Metric | Diagonal metric tensors have non-zero components only on the main diagonal. "There are also non-diagonal tensors which are generally associated with coordinate systems whose axes are not mutually orthogonal." |
| 21 | Positive Definite Metric | Riemann Geometry & Metric | A metric where all components are positive. "The metrics of Riemannian geometry are always positive definite, but the metrics of relativistic physics in general are not." |
| 22 | Geometric Invariance | Curvature & Geodesics | "The geometric properties of a manifold are invariant under coordinate transformations. Curvature and geodesics are two central properties of a manifold that are invariant." Both can be computed from the metric. |
| 23 | Geodesic | Curvature & Geodesics | Defined in two equivalent ways: (1) "the locally shortest path between two points on a given manifold, and which is contained in that manifold"; (2) "a locally straight line." The geodesic "generalises the notion of a straight line from Euclidean geometry." |
| 24 | Geodesic Integral Formula | Curvature & Geodesics | The distance between two fixed points a and b along a geodesic: l(a,b) = ∫_a^b √(Σ_i Σ_j g_ij dx_i dx_j) = ∫_a^b √(Σ_i Σ_j g_ij (dx_i/dt)(dx_j/dt)) dt, where t is a parameter. On a flat 2D manifold this reduces to the Euclidean distance formula. |
| 25 | Spherical Geodesics | Curvature & Geodesics | On the surface of a sphere, geodesics are "the great circles obtained as the intersection of the surface with a plane passing through its centre. In particular, the equator and the meridians are geodesic lines." |
| 26 | Three Curvature Classes | Curvature & Geodesics | "A manifold can have only three curvature classes: null, positive, and negative." Although surfaces of constant curvature are used to illustrate these classes, "in most situations, the curvature varies from point to point." |
| 27 | Parallel Postulate & Curvature | Curvature & Geodesics | The behavior of two initially parallel geodesics reveals the curvature class: (a) **null curvature** — geodesics keep mutual distance constant (parallel postulate holds); (b) **positive curvature** — geodesics converge; (c) **negative curvature** — geodesics diverge. |
| 28 | Single Geodesic Insufficiency | Curvature & Geodesics | "A single geodesic does not allow the type of curvature of a manifold to be established. To do this, two geodesics are required; in particular, it must be determined if the geodesics converge or diverge." |
| 29 | Geodesic Deviation ξ(t) | Curvature & Geodesics | "A measure of the curvature of a manifold." Defined as ξ(t) = x_A(t) − x_B(t), the separation between two geodesics A and B. "The more pronounced the deviation, the greater the curvature." If ξ(t) decreases → positive curvature; if ξ(t) increases → negative curvature; if ξ(t) remains constant → zero curvature (Euclidean). In Euclidean geometry and locally in non-Euclidean manifolds, the geodesic deviation is zero. |
| 30 | Local Geodesic Deviation | Curvature & Geodesics | "Locally, in a non-Euclidean manifold the geodesic deviation is also zero, since, in a small neighbourhood of a point, non-Euclidean manifolds agree with Euclidean geometry." |
| 31 | Non-Euclidean Criterion | Non-Euclidean Verification | "The basic criterion for establishing whether a manifold is non-Euclidean is to find disagreements with some results of Euclidean geometry," such as the sum of interior angles of a triangle, the perimeter/radius ratio of a circle, or the surface area of a sphere. |
| 32 | Euclidean Circumference/Radius Ratio | Non-Euclidean Verification | On a flat surface, integrating the flat 2D polar metric at constant r from 0 to 2π yields C = 2πr, so C/r = 2π — a well-known Euclidean result. |
| 33 | Spherical Circumference/Radius Ratio | Non-Euclidean Verification | On a spherical surface with constant θ, the perimeter is C = (r sinθ)·2π and the geodesic radius is ρ = rθ. The ratio is C/ρ = (sinθ/θ)·2π. Since sinθ/θ < 1 for θ > 0, C/ρ < 2π — a disagreement with Euclidean geometry proving the spherical surface is non-Euclidean. |
| 34 | Flat Spacetime (Special Relativity) | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | "This flat spacetime is the setting for the special theory of relativity (SR), which is named so because it represents a special case of GR in which there is no gravity." A four-dimensional manifold with three spatial and one temporal coordinate. |
| 35 | Spacetime as 4D Manifold | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | Spacetime is a four-dimensional manifold with "three spatial and one temporal coordinate." "Most of the ideas introduced [for Riemannian manifolds] apply to it, provided we define a geodesic as a locally straight line and keep in mind that the Riemannian manifolds are not exactly the same as the space-time manifolds." |
| 36 | Spacetime Interval ds² | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | The infinitesimal distance between two points in flat spacetime: ds² = dx_0² − (dx_1² + dx_2² + dx_3²) = dx_0² − dl², where x_0 ≡ ct is the time coordinate and c is the speed of light in vacuum. "ds is an invariant, but its interpretation is different" from the Riemannian dl. |
| 37 | Coordinate Time t | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | The time coordinate that "measures the lapse relative to a given coordinate system." Denoted as x_0 ≡ ct in the spacetime metric formulation. |
| 38 | Minkowski Metric | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | The flat spacetime metric: ds² = c²dt² − (dx² + dy² + dz²) in Cartesian spatial coordinates, or ds² = c²dt² − (dr² + r²dθ² + r²sin²θ dφ²) in spherical spatial coordinates. The fundamental metric of special relativity. |
| 39 | Greek Subscript Convention | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | In both SR and GR, "μ,ν = 0 denotes the temporal coordinate, and μ,ν = 1,2,3 denotes the spatial coordinates." This convention is used to write the metric tensor and metric in compact tensor form. |
| 40 | Minkowski Tensor η_μν | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | The 4×4 diagonal matrix with η_00=1, η_11=η_22=η_33=−1, and η_μν=0 if μ≠ν: diag(1,−1,−1,−1). "Describes the flat spacetime of SR." The Minkowski metric in tensor form: ds² = Σ_μ Σ_ν η_μν dx_μ dx_ν. |
| 41 | Proper Time τ | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | The time measured by an observer at rest in their own reference frame. For two events at the same place (dx=dy=dz=0): ds = cdt ≡ cdτ. τ "can be interpreted as the lapse measured by a 'wristwatch' that travels with the observer," and ds is the "displacement in time" made by the observer on their world line. |
| 42 | Null Geodesic | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | For a light pulse, dl = cdt, so ds = 0. "The light is said to describe a null geodesic in spacetime." A path in spacetime along which the spacetime interval is zero — the trajectory of light. |
| 43 | Pseudo-Riemannian Geometry | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | The geometry of relativistic spacetime. Unlike Riemannian geometry whose metrics are always positive definite, "the metrics of relativistic physics in general are not" positive definite — they have both positive and negative components. "It is said that the space-time of relativistic physics has pseudo-Riemannian geometry." The Minkowski tensor specifically describes a "pseudo-Euclidean" geometry (flat pseudo-Riemannian). |
| 44 | Signature | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | "A convention used in relativistic physics regarding the value of the signs (plus or minus) that accompany the components of the metric tensor." For diagonal metrics, the signature can be either (+,−,−,−) or (−,+,+,+), "without this choice altering the physical results derived from the metric." In Riemannian geometry the concept of signature is irrelevant since all components are positive. |
| 45 | Curved Spacetime Metric | Spacetime & Minkowski Geometry | "When spacetime is curved, the metric tensor takes a much more complicated form that typically depends on the gravitating masses that generate the curvature." Distinguished from the Minkowski tensor which describes flat spacetime. When curvature is zero everywhere, the metric tensor can always be brought to the simple diagonal form η_μν. |
| 46 | Physics–Mathematics Relationship | Foundations & Historical Context | "Since Galileo Galilei laid the foundations for the scientific method, physics and mathematics have maintained a close and fruitful relationship." Einstein's use of Riemannian geometry to describe gravity as curvature of spacetime is "one of the most emblematic examples." Marcel Grossman, a mathematician and close friend of Einstein, "introduced Einstein to the study of Riemannian geometry." |
